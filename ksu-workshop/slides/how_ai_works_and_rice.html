How AI Works & Writing Great Prompts

The Two Layers + The RICE Pattern

2026-07-27

What Happens When You Send a Prompt

You’re dealing with two layers, not one.

Two layers, stacked

The Model Layer

“The brain.” Knows a lot, knows nothing about you.

The App Layer

“The office.” Keeps your files and re-sends them.

Tell these apart and the “memory” features stop being magic.

The Model Layer

What the model itself can and can’t do

A brain with no memory of you

It has read most of the internet. It has read nothing about you.

  • Keeps: language, facts, math, reasoning
  • Forgets: you, your work, this conversation

So anything it should know about you, you hand it every time.

It predicts the next word

It does one thing, over and over: pick the next word, add it, ask again.

“The capital of Saudi Arabia ___”

  1. is (97%)
  2. was (2%)
  3. remains (0.6%)
  4. all other words … (0.2%)

“The capital of Saudi Arabia is ___”

  1. Riyadh (99%)
  2. Jeddah (0.4%)
  3. Mecca (0.3%)
  4. all other words … (0.2%)

“The capital of Saudi Arabia is Riyadh ___”

  1. , (52%)
  2. . (41%)
  3. : (4%)
  4. all other words … (2%)

“The capital of Saudi Arabia is Riyadh, a ___”

  1. city (61%)
  2. large (12%)
  3. major (8%)
  4. all other words … (6%)

At each step it knows which word usually comes next. That is the whole trick.

Percentages are illustrative.

When it guesses wrong

What happens when it doesn’t know the answer? It guesses.

“According to Article 214 of the new Investment Law: …”

  • The (45%) · Foreign (30%) · Capital (15%)

The sentence sounds right, and it has never read the document. That confident, made-up answer is a hallucination.

The App Layer

The office around the brain

We build an office around it

The brain can’t open files or remember your name. So we build an office around it: the app you actually use.

It keeps your information in files. Every time you ask, it gathers the relevant pages and hands them to the brain, then files the answer back.

The features you already use

“Memory”, “Projects”, “Custom Instructions” are all the app doing that gathering and re-sending for you.

The brain still has amnesia. The office is what makes it feel like it remembers.

What “Context” Really Is

The pages on the brain’s desk

What goes in front of the model

Context is what the office puts on the desk before the brain starts: your request, your instructions, the files you upload, and what came earlier in the chat.

What changes the answer most is what you put in the context.

The Desk Has Limits

The context window

Why long chats “forget”

Context space is limited. As a chat grows, there isn’t room for everything that came before, so the oldest of it drops out.

That’s the context window: a limit on what the model can take in for one request.

A Project saves your instructions and files

A Project in ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini is a saved workspace. Set it up once: name it, write your standing instructions, upload the files it should always see.

Every new chat inside it starts with those already loaded.

A “Course” Project. Standing instructions: always cite the syllabus, keep feedback constructive, use my rubric’s wording. Files uploaded once: the syllabus, the rubric, the reading list.

Micro-check

The AI cites a journal article that doesn’t exist. Which layer failed?

Both. The model guessed because the context held no real source. Give it the source, then check the citations.

RICE

Four elements that make your prompt clear: Role, Instructions, Context, Examples.

The framework

The harder the task, the more of these are worth adding.

R Role: the expert you’d hire.

I Instructions: the task, plus format, length, tone.

C Context: your notes, your files.

E Examples: one sample of “good”.

C is the same context from the two layers

You’re loading the pages onto the brain’s desk. No context = the brain guesses.

The Newspaper Test

C means real material leaves your desk. Before it does, ask: would I be comfortable seeing this text on the front page of a newspaper?

If not, strip names, student data, and unpublished work first. The answer is just as good without them.

This is Rule 1 of two. It guards what you paste in.

The formula

“Act as a [Role]. Your job is to [Instructions]. Here is the [Context]. Match this [Example].”

One good sample shows the shape you want faster than several lines of rules.

Delegate instead of asking

Don’t ask: “Write an email about the meeting.”

Delegate: “Act as a thesis advisor. Draft a recap email to my student from these notes. Highlight the revisions due before the next committee.”

Live Demo

Messy meeting notes → a clean student email

The messy notes

What you actually scribbled after the committee meeting:

“Met Sara re: thesis ch.3. Stats section weak, needs effect sizes, not just p-values. Lit review missing 2 key papers (ask her to add). Committee wants revised draft in 3 weeks. She’s nervous about the defense, reassure. Reminder: ethics form still not submitted.”

The RICE prompt

Role: Act as a supportive thesis advisor.

Instructions: Draft a warm, clear follow-up email to my student. Use these headers: Summary, Required Revisions, Deadline, Admin. Put the deadline in bold. End on an encouraging note. If anything is missing, add a “Questions for me” section.

Context: Notes from today’s committee meeting on Chapter 3: [paste notes]

Example: Match this opening: “Dear Sara, good progress this week. Here’s where we landed: …”

What made it work

  • Role → wrote as a mentor, not a robot
  • Instructions → the sections, the tone, the “Questions for me” rule
  • Context → messy notes were enough to extract the structure
  • Example → matched the warm, personal opening

Key takeaways

  1. Two layers: the model is the brain, the app is the office.
  2. The model predicts the next word, and can make things up.
  3. The answer follows the context. A Project keeps yours loaded.
  4. Brief it with RICE: Role, Instructions, Context, Examples.
  5. Run the Newspaper Test before you paste.
